Salmon Ruins

Salmon Ruins (Bloomfield, New Mexico) is home to an excavated archaeological pueblo structure first built in the 11th century with two continuous occupations, the Chacoan followed by the San Juan. The San Juan County Museum Association manages the ruins site with a visitor center and museum adjacent to the excavation site. The ruins site has intact structures including room blocks and kivas, while the museum houses numerous artifacts collected during the 1970s excavation.

The Salmon Ruins staff invited the Cultural SITEs team to scan both the ruins in their entirety and a selection of pottery artifacts using traditional photogrammetry techniques. A 3D model of the site was rendered into an interactive virtual tour for visitors who are unable to walk down to the ruins site itself. This virtual tour package, created using 3D Vista, included historic photographs from the original excavation together with an Artifact Catalog featuring high-resolution Gaussian Splat models of pottery artifacts accompanied by interpretive information.

BLOOMFIELD, NEW MEXICO— Salmon Pueblo is a Chacoan outlier pueblo located approximately 40–45 miles northeast of Chaco Canyon. The site was originally constructed in the 11th century by rapidly expanding Chaco populations, which makes it one of the most significant archaeological sites in the northwest region of New Mexico. Salmon Pueblo is characterized by two distinct, continuous occupations: the original Chacoan, followed by the San Juan, which is named for the San Juan River that runs nearby. The presence of two occupations is evidenced archaeologically by original Chacoan pueblo room blocks as well as additional kivas built on top of those structures during the San Juan period, creating a layered architectural record that distinguishes Salmon Ruins as a particularly rich and complex site for archaeological study.

The site was excavated in the 1970s by archaeologists who employed some of the most cutting-edge tools available in the discipline at that time. This legacy of technological experimentation and innovation made Salmon Ruins a natural and fitting location for Cultural SITEs' fifth and final site visit. The ruins themselves are largely accessible to the public; while portions of the site were backfilled following the original excavation, much of the pueblo remains visible and open, allowing visitors who are able to descend to the site to walk through many of the original room blocks. The San Juan County Museum Association manages the site and operates a robust museum and visitor center adjacent to the ruins, which houses a collection of upwards of two million artifacts recovered during the 1970s excavation, including an extensive catalog of Mimbres and Ancestral Puebloan pottery. Given the sheer volume of the collection, the museum is limited in how many artifacts it can display at any given time, making digital solutions for expanding public access to the collection a priority for the institution.

Given that ground-penetrating radar and magnetometry sensors had proven ineffective at the Mattocks Ruin scan, the team determined that subterranean data collection was not an appropriate data collection method for Salmon Ruins. Instead, the Cultural SITEs team focused on photogrammetry and full panoramic imaging with the goal of creating a comprehensive and interactive digital experience for museum visitors, particularly those who are unable to physically walk down the steep hill leading to the ruins or go down the steps into the excavated structures.

The team collected images of the structures with an aerial drone (DJI Mavic Pro), a DSLR camera, and a panoramic camera (Insta 360 Pro II). The resulting virtual experience was rendered using 3D Vista as the primary engine. Within this platform, the digital model of Salmon Ruins was developed into a highly interactive visitor experience in which users can navigate around the pueblo's room blocks and kivas, access interpretive information at key landmarks, and view archival photographs from the original 1970s archaeological excavation, including images of artifacts in situ. This creates a layered educational encounter that connects the present-day ruins to their excavation history.

In addition to the site itself, the SITEs team pursued artifact documentation in collaboration with Salmon Ruins Executive Director Tori Myers. Drawing on the initial experiments with Gaussian splatting techniques first used with the Torreón in Lincoln, the team photographed a selection of eight pottery artifacts from the museum's archive. These artifacts were rendered as high-resolution Gaussian Splats which were then incorporated into the 3D Vista interactive experience as a dedicated Artifact Catalog, allowing visitors to manipulate each object and view it from all angles alongside interpretive information. The result is one of the most successful and comprehensive outputs of the Cultural SITEs project, and the virtual experience is currently in active use at the Salmon Ruins Museum.
