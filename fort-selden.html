CULTURAL SCANS FOR INTERACTIVE 3D EXPERIENCES (SITES)

Fort Selden

Fort Selden Historic Site (Radium Springs, New Mexico) is a former United States government post established in 1865. Built out of adobe, this garrison was home to 1,800 soldiers over 25 years and served to keep law and order in Territorial southern New Mexico while assisting travelers through the region. Today Fort Selden is managed by New Mexico Historic Sites and comprises architectural remains that outline the footprint of the fort, along with artifacts, photographs, blueprints and other ephemera on view in the visitor center.

Fort Selden was the pilot historic location scanned for Cultural SITEs. The site ranged from foundations to a few adobe walls standing around 8 feet high. Using a DSLR camera and DJI Mavic drone for photogrammetric data collection, the SITEs team set out to render a complete digital exterior reconstruction of the Fort with one virtual interior reconstruction. This model now supports interactive public access and educational programming at the Visitor Center.

RADIUM SPRINGS, NEW MEXICO— Fort Selden Historic Site (managed by New Mexico Historic Sites) served as the pilot location for the Cultural SITEs project. In the spring of 2023, the New Mexico Humanities Council (NMHC) and Northrop Grumman's Technology for Conservation (T4C) initiative found a natural alignment in their shared vision for using photogrammetry and 3D imaging techniques to document and collect data on historic sites across New Mexico. The NMHC has a long institutional history of exploring the digital humanities and bridging the gap between technology and the humanities, while Northrop Grumman's T4C initiative is dedicated to leveraging the company's technological capabilities for both environmental and cultural conservation. With a Northrop Grumman office based in Albuquerque, the T4C team was particularly interested in pursuing cultural conservation and digital heritage work in their home state. The convergence of both organizations' interests made their partnership a natural fit.

Fort Selden presented a particularly compelling state of preservation. Constructed entirely out of adobe in the 1860s, the Fort's remaining walls currently stand at around 6–8 feet high in select areas, while much of the structure has been razed to the ground, leaving only the architectural footprint of the original buildings. This range of preservation provided the SITEs team with a rich opportunity to collect detailed data, including overhead aerial imagery and close-range textured photographs of the remaining structures.

The team was also able to draw on original blueprints and 19th-century archival photographs held by the New Mexico Historic Sites collection, combining this material with photogrammetric data to produce a digital reconstruction of the fort as it would have appeared in the 19th century. This was an ambitious undertaking that pushed the boundaries of photogrammetry as a tool for historic preservation and documentation, and the project took approximately a year and a half to complete.

The urgency of this kind of preservation work at Fort Selden is underscored by a land survey conducted in the 1970s, when New Mexico Historic Sites acquired the property. At that time, the adobe walls were eroding at a significant rate, and surveyors projected that the walls would be entirely gone by the 2020s. That projection prompted New Mexico Historic Sites to allocate resources toward installing structural supports for the remaining walls. Now well within that projected timeframe, the walls are still standing thanks to the site's management and preservation interventions. Fort Selden continues to benefit from ongoing documentation efforts that track the current condition and dimensions of the surviving structures.

Beyond physical documentation, the staff at Fort Selden placed a high priority on public access and the development of interactive visitor experiences. The SITEs team pursued two distinct experiential outputs: a virtual reality experience and an augmented reality application. The VR experience allows users to don a headset and walk through a digital reconstruction of the fort, including the interior of the officers' quarters, rendered using archival photographs of the original space.

The AR application offers two modes of engagement: on-site visitors can hold their phone up to the architectural remains on the parade grounds and see the digital reconstruction populate on their screens, conveying the original scale of the structure in situ. Remote users anywhere in the world can access the AR experience through a QR code link, bringing a model of the reconstructed fort directly to their device screens.

The complexity of this work yielded significant lessons for the SITEs team. Developing a digital reconstruction for immersive user experience proved to be an extraordinarily detailed and labor-intensive process, revealing just how much is involved in creating accurate, contextually grounded digital twins. For instance, the team discovered that faithfully representing the surrounding Robledo Mountains was essential to spatial orientation within the VR environment. Integrating the AR application with mapping platforms to ensure precise geographic placement presented additional technical challenges.

These discoveries fundamentally informed how the team approached data collection and output development at every subsequent site in the Cultural SITEs project.
